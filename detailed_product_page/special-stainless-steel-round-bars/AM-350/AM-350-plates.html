A refinery–power cogeneration site on the coast thought it had solved its fatigue and corrosion issues by switching from standard 410/420 stainless and quenched‑and‑tempered carbon steels to 316L plate in critical clamp rings, compressor shrouds and high‑load brackets, but vibration, thermal cycling and occasional chloride contamination kept producing cracks and unplanned outages in rotating equipment and hot gas housings. When the reliability and procurement teams redesigned these components in AM 350 Plates (UNS S35000), a precipitation‑hardening chromium–nickel–molybdenum stainless steel with a density around 7.94 g/cm³, they moved to a material whose annealed condition already delivers tensile strengths up to about 1105–1380 MPa, yield strengths around 415–585 MPa and a modulus of elasticity of 190–210 GPa, and whose cold‑worked plus aged conditions can exceed 1275–1380 MPa tensile and 1000–1035 MPa yield strength while maintaining usable ductility in service.

AM 350 (also designated Alloy 350 or AISI 633) is a precipitation‑hardening stainless steel that can develop a duplex austenitic–martensitic structure and combines high strength with good corrosion and oxidation resistance. It contains about 16–17% chromium, 4–5% nickel and 2.5–3.25% molybdenum with controlled carbon, manganese, silicon and nitrogen, and can be strengthened by either martensitic transformation plus precipitation hardening or by work hardening, giving designers a wide range of property combinations for plate, sheet and strip.

For procurement heads, equipment and corrosion engineers, fabricators, OEM manufacturers and hardware or industrial supply stores in chemical, oil & gas, marine, pharma and power‑generation sectors, AM 350 plates answer a specific problem: the need for significantly higher strength and fatigue resistance than 304/316 or 904L can offer, but without the brittle behaviour or limited weldability of many conventional martensitic steels. Data compiled for UNS S35000 show that precipitation‑hardened sheet and strip can achieve room‑temperature tensile strengths of around 185–200 ksi (≈1275–1380 MPa) and yield strengths of around 145–150 ksi (≈1000–1035 MPa) while maintaining enough elongation for forming and in‑service strain, and corrosion investigations report that AM 350 has pitting resistance numbers higher than some other precipitation‑hardening stainless steels and that suitably passivated AM 350 can operate in chloride and sulfuric‑acid environments with corrosion current densities on the order of 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵ mA/cm².

FAQ & Search Trends

Alloy descriptions stress that AM 350 combines very high tensile and yield strength with good corrosion resistance and weldability, using a precipitation‑hardening mechanism that does not depend on aluminium or titanium additions and therefore allows easier welding than many PH stainless steels. Compared with 304/316, UNS S35000 offers far higher strength and better fatigue resistance, permitting thinner, lighter sections, while compared with standard martensitic steels it maintains better toughness and corrosion resistance, especially where chloride and mild acid exposure coincide with high mechanical loads.

Engineers normally specify AM 350 as UNS S35000 or AISI 633 and cite ASTM A693 Grade 633 / ASME SA‑693 for plate, sheet and strip, alongside AMS 5548 and related AMS specifications for strip and sheet products.
