Why Waspaloy Sheet is Specified

Waspaloy is an age-hardenable nickel-chromium-cobalt superalloy: chromium 18.0–21.0% for oxidation resistance, cobalt 12.0–15.0% and molybdenum 3.5–5.0% in solid solution, and titanium 2.75–3.25% with aluminium 1.20–1.60% forming the gamma-prime that carries the strength. It holds high strength and creep resistance to roughly 870 °C (1,600 °F), which is why it has been a gas turbine material for decades.

In sheet gauges that combination behaves differently from the solid-solution alloys a fabricator is more used to. An Inconel 625 or Hastelloy sheet is bought for what it resists; a Waspaloy sheet is bought for what it still carries at temperature after the aging cycle. That makes the condition the sheet is delivered in as much a part of the order as the thickness, and it is the first thing to state on an enquiry.

Because the alloy is precipitation-hardened, forming and welding are normally done on solution-treated material and the aging cycle follows, so the finished part reaches full strength in its final shape rather than being fought into shape after it is hard. Sheet parts are then usually finish-machined or trimmed only lightly. Where the design calls for heavier sections – discs, spacers, machined rings – the same grade is ordered as Waspaloy plate or as forged bar and billet, which is what AMS 5704, 5706, 5707 and ASTM B637 cover.

Condition and Mechanical Properties

Waspaloy has no single set of room-temperature figures, because an age-hardenable alloy has the properties of the condition it is in. Solution-treated sheet is comparatively soft and formable; the same sheet after the aging cycle carries the strength and creep resistance the alloy is specified for. Quoting one number for both would describe neither.

So the acceptance limits that bind your material are the ones in AMS 5544 for the condition ordered, and the actual values for your heat are on the EN 10204 3.1 mill test certificate supplied with the material. Tell us the condition and the property your design is checked against, and we will confirm before the material ships rather than after.

FAQ & Search Trends

AMS 5544. Haynes International's Waspaloy brochure lists it against the "Sheet, Plate & Strip" row, so the same number covers plate. The AMS 5704 / 5706 / 5707 and ASTM B637 numbers often quoted alongside it are the billet, rod and bar specifications and do not apply to sheet; AMS 5828 is the welding rod and wire specification.

It depends where the forming and welding happen. Fabricators normally work the sheet in the solution-treated condition and run the aging cycle afterwards, so the finished part develops its strength in its final shape. If the part is machined only, aged material may suit. The condition changes the mechanical properties the certificate reports, so state it on the enquiry.

Both are age-hardenable nickel superalloys, but they harden by different routes: Waspaloy through gamma-prime from titanium 2.75–3.25% and aluminium 1.20–1.60% with cobalt 12.0–15.0% in solution, and Inconel 718 through gamma-double-prime from niobium. Waspaloy holds strength usefully higher up the temperature range, while 718 is easier to weld and is the more common choice below its own limit. Compare the two against the service temperature, not the room-temperature strength.
